Everything in Module 00 rested on one radio hearing another. This module gives you the vocabulary and the
arithmetic to reason about *whether* it will, and how much of the shared airwaves each message uses. All
numbers here are worked examples with stated inputs, not predictions for your location.

## Frequency and wavelength

**Frequency** is how many times per second the signal oscillates, in hertz (Hz). LoRa mesh radios in the US
use the 902–928 MHz band; other regions use other bands, which is why region and band come first in every setup. <Cite ids="REG-C04 HW-C01" />

**Wavelength** (λ) is the distance one oscillation covers: λ = c / f, where c ≈ 299,792,458 m/s.

- At 915 MHz: λ = 299,792,458 / 915,000,000 ≈ **0.328 m** (about 33 cm).
- At 868 MHz: λ ≈ **0.345 m**. <Cite ids="RF-C10" />

Antennas are built for a band. A 915 MHz antenna on an 868 MHz radio, or no antenna at all, wastes signal and
can damage some transmitters. <Cite ids="HW-C09" /> Module 02 covers this for specific boards.

## Antennas, gain and polarization

An antenna doesn't add power. **Gain**, in dBi, means it concentrates the same power in some directions
at the expense of others, compared with an ideal antenna radiating equally in every direction. A higher-gain
antenna is not automatically better: it may point energy away from where your neighbors are.

**Polarization** is the orientation of the signal's electric field. Two vertical antennas match; one vertical
and one horizontal lose signal to the mismatch. Simple path-loss formulas assume matched polarization. <Cite ids="RF-C14" />

## Line of sight and Fresnel clearance

Seeing the other antenna is not the whole story. The radio energy travels in a fat, football-shaped region
around the straight line between antennas, called the **first Fresnel zone**. The ITU's diffraction
recommendation treats a path as plain line of sight only when no obstacle sits inside this zone. Obstacles
that poke into it (rooftops, trees, terrain) can add loss even when you have visual line of sight.

Its radius at a point d1 from one end and d2 from the other is r = √(λ · d1 · d2 / (d1 + d2))
(ITU-R P.526-16, Annex 1 §2.1, eq. 2, with n = 1). <Cite ids="RF-C11 RF-S05" />

**Worked example.** A 2 km link at 915 MHz, measured at the midpoint (d1 = d2 = 1000 m):
r = √(0.328 × 1000 × 1000 / 2000) ≈ **12.8 m**. The zone is about 12.8 m in radius halfway along, which is one
reason antenna height matters so much. This is geometry, not a coverage prediction.

## Decibels: dB and dBm

Radio power spans enormous ranges, so engineers use logarithms.

- **dB** is a *ratio*: 10 × log10(P2 / P1). +3 dB is about double, +10 dB is ten times, −3 dB about half.
- **dBm** is an *absolute* power relative to 1 milliwatt: 0 dBm = 1 mW. <Cite ids="RF-C09" />

| dBm | Power |
|---|---|
| 0 dBm | 1 mW |
| 10 dBm | 10 mW |
| 14 dBm | ≈ 25 mW |
| 17 dBm | ≈ 50 mW |
| 20 dBm | 100 mW |
| 30 dBm | 1 W |

Adding dB to dBm gives dBm: 14 dBm + 3 dB = 17 dBm, roughly doubling 25 mW to 50 mW. Adding two dBm values
directly is a common mistake. It doesn't mean anything.

## Worked example: a link budget

A **link budget** adds up every gain and loss between transmitter and receiver, in dB. The values below are
synthetic teaching inputs. <Cite ids="RF-C07" />

<LinkBudget />

- Received power: 14 + 2 − 1 − 115 + 2 − 1 = **−99 dBm**.
- The receiver's **sensitivity** is the weakest signal it can decode at given settings. Here we *assume*
  −120 dBm. Real sensitivity depends on the chip and on spreading factor and bandwidth.
- **Link margin** = received − sensitivity = −99 − (−120) = **21 dB** of headroom.
- Add 25 dB of extra assumed loss, for a wall of trees say: received becomes −124 dBm and the margin **−4 dB**.
  The model now says the link is below the assumed sensitivity.

Where did 115 dB of path loss come from? It is an assumed number. For comparison, the free-space formula
gives about **91.6 dB** at 915 MHz over 1 km with nothing in the way. <Cite ids="RF-C06" /> Real paths add terrain,
buildings, foliage and fading on top. A positive margin is a planning signal, not a promise of delivery or range,
and it says nothing about whether a power or antenna setting is legal.

## RSSI and SNR: what the radio reports

- **RSSI** (received signal strength indicator) is the radio's estimate of received power, in dBm.
- **SNR** (signal-to-noise ratio) compares the signal with the background noise, in dB. <Cite ids="RF-C12" />

Why a strong RSSI isn't enough:

1. **Collisions.** Two transmitters that can't hear each other may send at the same moment. A receiver
   between them hears plenty of energy but decodes neither packet. These are called **hidden transmitters**.
   <Cite ids="RF-C13" />
2. **Wrong settings or protocol.** A strong signal on mismatched settings, or in a different mesh protocol,
   still doesn't become a message (Module 00).
3. **Wrong key or routing.** A packet can arrive intact and still not be readable or not be meant for you.

You can see hidden transmitters on the [routing playground](../../labs/routing-playground/): untick "B and C can
hear each other" and watch C transmit even though B already did. The playground doesn't model collisions,
but in a real network those two transmissions could overlap at D.

## Airtime: what makes a packet take longer

Every packet occupies the channel for a while, and everyone nearby shares that channel. Longer airtime means
more chance of overlapping with someone else and more battery used. The classic Semtech formula for SX1276-class
radios ties airtime to these inputs: <Cite ids="RF-C01 RF-C02" />

| Input | Effect on airtime |
|---|---|
| **Spreading factor (SF)** | Each step up doubles the symbol time (Ts = 2^SF / BW). More robust, much slower. <Cite ids="MT-C27" /> |
| **Bandwidth (BW)** | Doubling the bandwidth halves the symbol time. Faster, but the Meshtastic docs note less link budget. <Cite ids="MT-C27" /> |
| **Coding rate (CR)** | 4/5 to 4/8 adds redundancy bits that help against noise and cost more symbols. |
| **Preamble** | Extra symbols sent before the data so the receiver can lock on. |
| **Explicit header and CRC** | The header describes the packet; the CRC lets the receiver check it. Both add bits. |
| **Payload bytes** | More physical bytes means more symbols. Mesh headers and encryption add bytes on top of your text. <Cite ids="RF-C05" /> |
| **Hops and retries** | Each relay and each retry is a whole extra packet on the air. |

**Worked example.** 20 physical bytes, SF7, 125 kHz, CR 4/5, 8-symbol preamble, explicit header, CRC on:

- Symbol time Ts = 2⁷ / 125,000 = **1.024 ms**.
- The formula gives 43 payload symbols, plus 8 + 4.25 preamble symbols.
- Airtime = (12.25 + 43) × 1.024 ms = **56.576 ms**. Ten identical packets: **565.76 ms**. <Cite ids="RF-C03" />

The same packet at **SF12** (low-data-rate optimization on, which the datasheet requires when a symbol lasts
over 16 ms) takes **1318.912 ms**, over 23 times longer. <Cite ids="RF-C04 RF-C02" />

Note that 20 *physical* bytes is not a 20-character message. The mesh protocol adds its own header and
cryptographic bytes before the radio sees them.

## Predict before you check

Starting from the SF7 example, change **one** thing at a time. Rank these by airtime, shortest first:
(a) SF8, (b) bandwidth 250 kHz, (c) coding rate 4/8.

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

(b) 250 kHz: **28.288 ms**, half, because symbol time halves.
(c) CR 4/8: **78.08 ms**, 64 payload symbols instead of 43.
(a) SF8: **102.912 ms**. Symbol time doubles to 2.048 ms, but only 38 payload symbols are needed, so it is
a bit less than double. <Cite ids="RF-C15" />

If you guessed SF8 is exactly double, you've spotted the main effect. The payload symbol count also shifts
slightly with SF.
</details>

## Knowledge check

<details>
<summary>1. A radio transmits at 20 dBm into a cable with 2 dB loss and an antenna with 3 dBi gain. What leaves the antenna, in dBm?</summary>

20 − 2 + 3 = **21 dBm**. Gains and losses in dB add to a dBm starting value. Note that what counts toward a
legal limit depends on the specific rule and device authorization, which a later module covers.
</details>

<details>
<summary>2. Your radio shows a strong RSSI from a neighbor's node, but your messages to them keep failing. Name two possible causes that RSSI cannot reveal.</summary>

Any two of: collisions with a hidden transmitter; a different mesh protocol; mismatched channel or key;
routing problems further along; the neighbor's reply path failing. RSSI only says energy arrived. <Cite ids="RF-C12" />
</details>

<details>
<summary>3. A link has a 21 dB margin on paper. Does that mean messages will always get through?</summary>

No. The margin rests on assumed path loss and sensitivity. Real paths add obstruction, fading and
interference; traffic adds collisions. It is a planning number to compare designs, not a guarantee.
</details>

<details>
<summary>4. Two hilltops are in plain view of each other, but the midpoint of the path skims a ridge 5 m below the straight line. Why might the link still be weak?</summary>

The ridge sits inside the first Fresnel zone, which here is many meters wide. Visual line of sight is not
the same as a clear Fresnel zone. <Cite ids="RF-C11" />
</details>

<details>
<summary>Engineering detail: the full airtime expression</summary>

From the Semtech SX1276/77/78/79 datasheet (Rev. 4, §4.1.1.7), with BW in Hz, CR = 1…4 for 4/5…4/8, PL the
physical payload in bytes, IH = 1 for implicit header, CRC = 1 when on, DE = 1 for low-data-rate optimization:

```text
Ts        = 2^SF / BW
Npayload  = 8 + max( ceil( (8·PL − 4·SF + 28 + 16·CRC − 20·IH) / (4·(SF − 2·DE)) ) · (CR + 4), 0 )
Tpacket   = (Npreamble + 4.25 + Npayload) · Ts
```

This form is checked for SF7–SF12 on SX1276-class radios. Other chips (SX1262, LR1110) support further
modes that need their own references. Semtech's LoRaMac-node driver rounds the result up to whole
milliseconds (57 ms and 1319 ms for the two examples above). <Cite ids="RF-C01 RF-C03 RF-C04" />

The Meshtastic 2.7 docs summarize the same trade-offs: each SF step roughly doubles airtime and adds about
2.5 dB of link budget; their preset table assumes 22 dBm and a 0 dBi antenna. <Cite ids="MT-C27" />
</details>
